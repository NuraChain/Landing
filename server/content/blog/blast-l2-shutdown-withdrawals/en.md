Blast, an Ethereum Layer 2 that once held $2.26 billion, announced on 2 October 2026 that it is shutting down. Users have until 26 October to withdraw their assets to Ethereum through the normal bridge. After that date funds can still be recovered, but only by calling Blast's bridge contracts on Ethereum directly. The team's reason was short: "The ongoing costs of maintaining Blast exceed the revenue generated by the L2, and we do not see a credible path to making the chain economically sustainable."

## Key facts

- **Announced:** 2 October 2026.
- **Deadline:** withdraw to Ethereum by 26 October 2026.
- **After the deadline:** withdrawals only by interacting with the bridge contracts on Ethereum mainnet.
- **A pause first:** the team is withdrawing Blast's assets from Lido, expected to take about a week, and user withdrawals are unavailable during it.
- **Then:** withdrawals reopen with a 24-hour delay.
- **The fall:** total value locked peaked at $2.26 billion in June 2024 and was $24.7 million at the announcement.

## Where are your funds on a Layer 2?

On Ethereum, in a bridge contract.

When you deposit to a rollup, your ETH is locked in a contract on Ethereum and the rollup credits you a balance. That balance is a claim on the locked funds. Withdrawing means proving your claim to the contract on Ethereum, which then releases the ETH.

This is why a shutdown is survivable. The assets were never on the Layer 2 in the sense of being held there; they are held on Ethereum, and the contract that holds them keeps existing after the Layer 2 stops producing blocks.

## Why is there a deadline, then?

Because the easy route depends on the operator.

The website, the bridge interface and the sequencer that orders transactions are all run by the team. While they run, withdrawing is a button. Once they stop, the claim remains but exercising it means calling a contract yourself — something most users have never done and many will get wrong.

Blast adds a complication. It put deposited ETH into Lido to earn yield, so the funds have to be unstaked before they can be paid out. That is the week-long pause.

## What should a Blast user do?

- **Withdraw through the official bridge** once it reopens, well before 26 October.
- **Reach it from a bookmark,** not a link. Shutdowns attract phishing sites that copy the real interface.
- **Check tokens as well as ETH.** A token issued only on Blast has nowhere to go; a bridged one does.
- **Unwind positions first.** Funds in a lending or liquidity contract on Blast must be withdrawn from it before they can be bridged.

## Is this going to keep happening?

Yes. A Layer 2 has running costs — sequencing, posting data to Ethereum, engineering — and its revenue is the fees its users pay. With few users the sums do not work, and activity has been concentrating on a handful of networks. Analysts at 21Shares forecast that most Ethereum Layer 2s would not survive 2026.

The number of chains was never the hard part, as [why EVM chains keep multiplying](/blog/why-so-many-evm-chains) argues. Keeping one alive is.

## What should you ask of any chain you use?

- **Where is the asset really held,** and what contract releases it?
- **Can you exit without the operator?** If so, how, and has anyone tested it?
- **Who pays to keep it running,** and from what?

Those questions apply to Nura Chain as much as to a rollup. NURA is the network's native coin; its bridged BNB and USDT are claims on the bridge that minted them, and you can read their supply on [the explorer](/blog/how-to-use-nura-chain-explorer). Reading the supply is not an exit guarantee, and this page does not offer one.
