The CLARITY Act — the bill meant to settle which US regulator oversees which crypto asset — failed its first Senate test on 15 September 2026. The vote to open debate went 49 to 50, eleven short of the 60 it needed. The House had passed the bill in July 2025 by 294 to 134. The bill is not formally dead, but a market structure law in the United States is now unlikely before 2027.

## Key facts

- **The vote:** cloture on the motion to proceed, 15 September 2026, failed 49–50 with 60 required.
- **Why it is not over on paper:** Senator Thom Tillis switched to "no", which lets him move to reconsider and bring it back.
- **The sticking point:** whether stablecoin holders can be paid rewards, which banks see as competition for deposits.
- **What still stands:** the GENIUS Act for payment stablecoins, which takes effect on 18 January 2027.

## What would the CLARITY Act have decided?

Who is in charge.

The bill divides authority between the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, and sets a test for when a token is a commodity rather than a security. Today that question is answered case by case, after the fact, usually in an enforcement action. The CFTC also lacks direct authority over spot markets in crypto commodities, so the venues where most trading happens sit in a gap between two agencies.

A statute would have turned that into something a project could read before it launched.

## Why did it fail?

Not over the central idea. The fight was about stablecoin yield.

Banks argued that if a stablecoin can pay something that looks like interest, deposits will leave for it. Several Republican senators said they would not support the bill while that worried the banks, and Senator Cynthia Lummis, a leading Republican negotiator, said Democrats had "not budged an inch". Neither side had the votes without the other.

So a bill about market structure stalled on a question about what a dollar token is allowed to pay — which says something about where the commercial stakes actually are.

## What does the failure change in practice?

Less than a headline about a failed vote suggests, and it is worth being exact.

- **Nothing became illegal.** The vote leaves the law where it was.
- **Stablecoin rules are unaffected.** They come from a different act, already passed — see [what the GENIUS Act changes onchain](/blog/genius-act-stablecoin-rules).
- **Regulators keep acting through rules and guidance,** which the next set of appointees can revise. That is the real cost: a rule is easier to reverse than a statute.
- **Other jurisdictions are not waiting.** The EU's regime is fully in force, and South Korea has dated its own.

## What should a builder or holder do with this?

Read it as a statement about one country's timetable, not about what a chain can do. A blockchain executes the same code whether or not a legislature has classified the token. Nura Chain is an EVM network like any other in that respect: what a contract does is defined by its bytecode, which is public, and [how to read the Nura Chain explorer](/blog/how-to-use-nura-chain-explorer) shows how to check it yourself.

What law decides is who may offer an asset to whom, and under what disclosures. If you are issuing a token to US residents, that is a question for a lawyer, and this vote means the answer stays unsettled for longer.
