On Saturday 5 September 2026, DBS in Singapore and Citi in New York settled a US dollar payment between them in minutes. That is unremarkable for anyone who has sent a stablecoin and remarkable for a bank: a cross-border dollar payment normally waits for both banking systems to be open, and across a weekend that can mean two business days. The payment used tokenized deposits on the blockchain-based ledger Swift is building.

## Key facts

- **What happened:** the first weekend cross-border US dollar payment using tokenized deposits on Swift's ledger, on 5 September 2026.
- **Who:** DBS in Singapore and Citi in New York.
- **How long:** minutes, against up to two business days over a weekend.
- **Scale of the pilot:** 17 banks are testing round-the-clock payments on the ledger, among them ANZ, BNP Paribas, HSBC and UBS.

## What is a tokenized deposit?

It is a bank deposit, recorded as a token.

The money stays on the issuing bank's balance sheet. It is still a claim on that bank, still covered by the rules the bank already operates under, and still only as good as that bank. What changes is the record: instead of an entry in the bank's own database, the claim is a token on a shared ledger that other banks can read and settle against at any hour.

## How is that different from a stablecoin?

In who owes you the money, and who is allowed to hold it.

- **A stablecoin** is a claim on an issuer who holds reserves, and it circulates on public chains where anyone with a wallet can hold it. The rules for those issuers are what [the GENIUS Act](/blog/genius-act-stablecoin-rules) sets out.
- **A tokenized deposit** is a claim on a bank, inside a network of banks. You cannot withdraw it to a self-custody wallet, and it was never meant to leave the system it was issued in.

Both settle in minutes on a weekend. They answer the same complaint from opposite directions: stablecoins put dollars on open networks and regulation followed; tokenized deposits start from regulated money and add the ledger.

## Why does a weekend matter so much?

Because the delay was never technical. Cross-border payments wait on cut-off times, on correspondent banks, and on two countries' working hours overlapping. A ledger both banks can write to at any time removes the waiting without removing the banks.

That is also the honest limit of it. The speed comes from the shared ledger; the trust still comes from knowing the counterparty is a regulated bank. It is a faster version of the existing system rather than an alternative to it.

## What does this mean for public chains?

That the competition has changed. For years the argument for moving dollars on a public chain was that the banking system could not do it on a Saturday. One payment in a pilot does not end that argument, but it shows the banks intend to.

What public chains still offer is the part a bank network does not: anyone can hold the asset, anyone can build on it, and settlement takes the same few seconds for a stranger as for a member. On an EVM network that is an ordinary token transfer — [what gas actually costs in 2026](/blog/what-gas-actually-costs-in-2026) covers what such a transfer costs.
